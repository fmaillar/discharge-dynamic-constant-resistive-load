* COMMENT Locked scientific scope

** Central question

Do archived Li-ion discharges obtained under two constant resistive loads admit
a simple global temporal-rate scaling, and how does the local voltage-rate
scaling vary along the discharge trajectory?

** Primary result to test and report

For the two discharge families,

\[
\left(\frac{dV}{dt}\right)_{3.33\,\Omega}
\approx
\alpha
\left(\frac{dV}{dt}\right)_{5\,\Omega},
\]

with the fitted global factor experimentally equal to the load-resistance ratio,

\[
\alpha \simeq \frac{5}{3.33}.
\]

The present analysis gives \(\alpha=1.498019\) and
\(5/3.33=1.501502\), a relative difference of approximately 0.23%.

** Local quantity

The local dynamic ratio is

\[
K(V)=
\frac{
\left(dV/dt\right)_{3.33\,\Omega}
}{
\left(dV/dt\right)_{5\,\Omega}
}.
\]

The central secondary observation is that \(K(V)\) is not constant over the
robust voltage interval even though the global scale factor follows the
resistance ratio.

** Claims allowed by the archived data

- A global scaling factor can be estimated reproducibly from the two discharge
  families.
- The global factor is experimentally indistinguishable, at the precision
  relevant here, from the ratio of load resistances.
- The local dynamic ratio varies systematically with voltage.
- The form of the local ratio is robust to the tested smoothing windows and
  derivative thresholds.
- Bootstrap and leave-one-out analyses can quantify repeatability and
  sensitivity to the finite set of repeated discharges.

** Claims explicitly excluded

- The local variation of \(K(V)\) is not attributed uniquely to load
  resistance because load condition and acquisition order are confounded.
- No microscopic electrochemical mechanism is identified from these data alone.
- No universal scaling law across cell chemistries, temperatures, ageing states,
  or load families is claimed.
- =current_a= is not treated as an independent measurement; for these processed
  files it is derived from terminal voltage and the known resistive load.
- The work does not revisit the harmonic-fluctuation, aliasing, or low-SOC
  spectral claims of the previous article.

** Separation from the previous article

The previous article studied fast detrended voltage fluctuations, harmonic
aliasing, phase locking, periodic reconstruction, and low-SOC spectral
organization. The present manuscript uses the slow discharge trajectory and its
voltage-rate field. Harmonic spectra, aliased lines, periodic reconstruction,
and spectral-noise conclusions are outside the present contribution.

* Experimental data and acquisition

** Discharge data set

The analysis uses ten archived discharge records, D70--D79, from the same
experimental campaign analysed in the previous study. The records form two
constant-resistance groups: D70--D74 were acquired with a \(3.33\,\Omega\)
load and D75--D79 with a \(5\,\Omega\) load, giving five repeated
discharges for each load condition. All ten records were sampled at
\(f_s=10\,\mathrm{Hz}\). The nominal reference capacity stored in the
archived metadata is \(2.6\,\mathrm{Ah}\).

For the present work, the primary experimental observables are time and
reconstructed terminal voltage. The archived files also contain SOC and a
derived current channel, but the analysis developed here is formulated directly
in the voltage domain and does not require SOC as its independent coordinate.
This choice distinguishes the present study from the previous low-SOC spectral
analysis: the discharge trajectories are compared at common terminal-voltage
values rather than partitioned primarily by SOC.

The ten records are treated as two finite experimental populations of repeated
discharges. No additional cell types, temperatures, ageing states, or loading
protocols are introduced. Conclusions are therefore restricted to these archived
discharge families.

** Measurement chain

Terminal voltage was acquired with a Keithley 2182 nanovoltmeter. In the
archived processed records, the battery-voltage channel had already been
converted to terminal voltage using the divider gain stored with the data; for
D70--D79 the archived divider gain is 500. The present analysis therefore uses
the reconstructed terminal-voltage signal provided by the archived data rather
than attempting a new reconstruction from raw instrument counts.

As in the previous study, only acquisition parameters directly supported by the
archived records and metadata are reported. The surviving acquisition material
does not permit an unambiguous reconstruction of every instrument setting used
for D70--D79. Settings found in auxiliary scripts are therefore not assumed to
represent these ten measurements unless they are independently supported by the
archived data.

This restriction is intentional. The present work relies on relative comparisons
between repeated discharge trajectories acquired within the same experimental
campaign, and no inference is based on undocumented instrument settings.

** Constant-resistance loading and derived current

The two experimental conditions differ by the external resistive load,
\(R_L=3.33\,\Omega\) and \(R_L=5\,\Omega\). Under an ideal resistive
load, the instantaneous current associated with the measured terminal voltage
is

\[
I(t)=\frac{V(t)}{R_L}.
\]

The =current_a= field in the processed discharge files is not an independent
current measurement; it was reconstructed from terminal voltage and the known
load resistance according to the expression above. Consequently, current is not
used as an independent observable in the present analysis. In particular,
voltage--current correlations or coherence would be deterministic consequences
of the reconstruction and are not used as physical evidence.

The load resistance is instead treated as the experimental condition. This
makes the natural first-order scaling expectation explicit: at a common terminal
voltage, the ideal resistive-load current ratio is

\[
\frac{I_{3.33}}{I_{5}}
=
\frac{V/3.33}{V/5}
=
\frac{5}{3.33}.
\]

The scaling analyses introduced below test whether the measured voltage-rate
dynamics exhibit the same global ratio and whether departures from that global
relation depend systematically on terminal voltage.

** Sequential acquisition design

The acquisition order is an intrinsic limitation of the archived experiment.
The \(3.33\,\Omega\) records are D70--D74 and the \(5\,\Omega\)
records are D75--D79; the two load conditions were therefore not interleaved or
randomized. Load condition and acquisition order are consequently confounded.

This design does not prevent comparison of the two archived discharge families,
but it limits causal interpretation of local differences between them. A
voltage-dependent difference cannot be attributed uniquely to load resistance if
an unobserved sequential change in the experimental system could produce a
comparable effect.

For that reason, the present study separates two questions. The first is whether
the two discharge families exhibit a reproducible global scaling relation. The
second is whether the local departure from this global scaling varies with
voltage. The latter is reported as an empirical property of the two sequential
data sets rather than as proof of a resistance-only causal mechanism.

The possible influence of sequential order is evaluated explicitly later using
within-group trends and leave-one-out analyses. No attempt is made to compensate
for the acquisition order by introducing an unverified correction model.
