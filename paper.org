* COMMENT Locked scientific scope

** Central question

Do archived Li-ion discharges obtained under two constant resistive loads admit
a simple global temporal-rate scaling, and how does the local voltage-rate
scaling vary along the discharge trajectory?

** Primary result to test and report

For the two discharge families,

\[
\left(\frac{dV}{dt}\right)_{3.33\,\Omega}
\approx
\alpha
\left(\frac{dV}{dt}\right)_{5\,\Omega},
\]

with the fitted global factor experimentally equal to the load-resistance ratio,

\[
\alpha \simeq \frac{5}{3.33}.
\]

The present analysis gives \(\alpha=1.498019\) and
\(5/3.33=1.501502\), a relative difference of approximately 0.23%.

** Local quantity

The local dynamic ratio is

\[
K(V)=
\frac{
\left(dV/dt\right)_{3.33\,\Omega}
}{
\left(dV/dt\right)_{5\,\Omega}
}.
\]

The central secondary observation is that \(K(V)\) is not constant over the
robust voltage interval even though the global scale factor follows the
resistance ratio.

** Claims allowed by the archived data

- A global scaling factor can be estimated reproducibly from the two discharge
  families.
- The global factor is experimentally indistinguishable, at the precision
  relevant here, from the ratio of load resistances.
- The local dynamic ratio varies systematically with voltage.
- The form of the local ratio is robust to the tested smoothing windows and
  derivative thresholds.
- Bootstrap and leave-one-out analyses can quantify repeatability and
  sensitivity to the finite set of repeated discharges.

** Claims explicitly excluded

- The local variation of \(K(V)\) is not attributed uniquely to load
  resistance because load condition and acquisition order are confounded.
- No microscopic electrochemical mechanism is identified from these data alone.
- No universal scaling law across cell chemistries, temperatures, ageing states,
  or load families is claimed.
- =current_a= is not treated as an independent measurement; for these processed
  files it is derived from terminal voltage and the known resistive load.
- The work does not revisit the harmonic-fluctuation, aliasing, or low-SOC
  spectral claims of the previous article.

** Separation from the previous article

The previous article studied fast detrended voltage fluctuations, harmonic
aliasing, phase locking, periodic reconstruction, and low-SOC spectral
organization. The present manuscript uses the slow discharge trajectory and its
voltage-rate field. Harmonic spectra, aliased lines, periodic reconstruction,
and spectral-noise conclusions are outside the present contribution.

* Experimental data and acquisition

** Discharge data set

The analysis uses ten archived discharge records, D70--D79, from the same
experimental campaign analysed in the previous study. The records form two
constant-resistance groups: D70--D74 were acquired with a \(3.33\,\Omega\)
load and D75--D79 with a \(5\,\Omega\) load, giving five repeated
discharges for each load condition. All ten records were sampled at
\(f_s=10\,\mathrm{Hz}\). The nominal reference capacity stored in the
archived metadata is \(2.6\,\mathrm{Ah}\).

For the present work, the primary experimental observables are time and
reconstructed terminal voltage. The archived files also contain SOC and a
derived current channel, but the analysis developed here is formulated directly
in the voltage domain and does not require SOC as its independent coordinate.
This choice distinguishes the present study from the previous low-SOC spectral
analysis: the discharge trajectories are compared at common terminal-voltage
values rather than partitioned primarily by SOC.

The ten records are treated as two finite experimental populations of repeated
discharges. No additional cell types, temperatures, ageing states, or loading
protocols are introduced. Conclusions are therefore restricted to these archived
discharge families.

** Measurement chain

Terminal voltage was acquired with a Keithley 2182 nanovoltmeter. In the
archived processed records, the battery-voltage channel had already been
converted to terminal voltage using the divider gain stored with the data; for
D70--D79 the archived divider gain is 500. The present analysis therefore uses
the reconstructed terminal-voltage signal provided by the archived data rather
than attempting a new reconstruction from raw instrument counts.

As in the previous study, only acquisition parameters directly supported by the
archived records and metadata are reported. The surviving acquisition material
does not permit an unambiguous reconstruction of every instrument setting used
for D70--D79. Settings found in auxiliary scripts are therefore not assumed to
represent these ten measurements unless they are independently supported by the
archived data.

This restriction is intentional. The present work relies on relative comparisons
between repeated discharge trajectories acquired within the same experimental
campaign, and no inference is based on undocumented instrument settings.

** Constant-resistance loading and derived current

The two experimental conditions differ by the external resistive load,
\(R_L=3.33\,\Omega\) and \(R_L=5\,\Omega\). Under an ideal resistive
load, the instantaneous current associated with the measured terminal voltage
is

\[
I(t)=\frac{V(t)}{R_L}.
\]

The =current_a= field in the processed discharge files is not an independent
current measurement; it was reconstructed from terminal voltage and the known
load resistance according to the expression above. Consequently, current is not
used as an independent observable in the present analysis. In particular,
voltage--current correlations or coherence would be deterministic consequences
of the reconstruction and are not used as physical evidence.

The load resistance is instead treated as the experimental condition. This
makes the natural first-order scaling expectation explicit: at a common terminal
voltage, the ideal resistive-load current ratio is

\[
\frac{I_{3.33}}{I_{5}}
=
\frac{V/3.33}{V/5}
=
\frac{5}{3.33}.
\]

The scaling analyses introduced below test whether the measured voltage-rate
dynamics exhibit the same global ratio and whether departures from that global
relation depend systematically on terminal voltage.

** Sequential acquisition design

The acquisition order is an intrinsic limitation of the archived experiment.
The \(3.33\,\Omega\) records are D70--D74 and the \(5\,\Omega\)
records are D75--D79; the two load conditions were therefore not interleaved or
randomized. Load condition and acquisition order are consequently confounded.

This design does not prevent comparison of the two archived discharge families,
but it limits causal interpretation of local differences between them. A
voltage-dependent difference cannot be attributed uniquely to load resistance if
an unobserved sequential change in the experimental system could produce a
comparable effect.

For that reason, the present study separates two questions. The first is whether
the two discharge families exhibit a reproducible global scaling relation. The
second is whether the local departure from this global scaling varies with
voltage. The latter is reported as an empirical property of the two sequential
data sets rather than as proof of a resistance-only causal mechanism.

The possible influence of sequential order is evaluated explicitly later using
within-group trends and leave-one-out analyses. No attempt is made to compensate
for the acquisition order by introducing an unverified correction model.

* Methods

** Slow voltage trajectory and voltage-rate estimation

For each discharge, the terminal-voltage signal \(V(t)\) was smoothed with a
Savitzky--Golay polynomial filter before estimating the slow voltage-rate field.
The default processing used a \(60\,\mathrm{s}\) window and a polynomial
order of 3. At the archived sampling frequency of \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\),
this corresponds to approximately 600 samples; the implementation enforces an
odd window length as required by the filter.

The slow voltage derivative was obtained directly from the differentiated
Savitzky--Golay polynomial rather than by finite differencing the smoothed
signal. For each record,

\[
\dot V(t)
=
\frac{dV}{dt},
\]

was evaluated with the same polynomial order and window as the smoothed
trajectory, using the physical sampling interval
\(\Delta t=1/f_s\). To reduce edge effects introduced by the moving
polynomial fit, half of the filter-window length was discarded at each end
before interpolation or comparison between discharges.

The present analysis intentionally targets the slow discharge trajectory. It
does not use the detrended fast residual employed in the previous spectral
study, and no spectral decomposition is required here.

** Common voltage coordinate

The discharge records have different durations and therefore cannot be compared
pointwise on their native time axes. Instead, each processed derivative
trajectory was expressed as a function of terminal voltage,
\(\dot V(V)\).

For every discharge, the smoothed-voltage samples were sorted by voltage and the
corresponding derivative values were interpolated onto a common voltage grid.
The grid was restricted to the voltage interval shared by all ten processed
records and contained 1000 equally spaced points in the default analysis.
Duplicate voltage values, when present numerically, were collapsed before
interpolation.

For load group \(R\), the repeated trajectories were then combined by the
pointwise arithmetic mean,

\[
\overline{\dot V}_{R}(V)
=
\frac{1}{N_R}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_R}
\dot V_{R,j}(V),
\]

with \(N_R=5\) for each resistance condition. This voltage-coordinate
representation allows the two load groups to be compared at the same terminal
voltage independently of their different discharge durations.

** Global scaling factor

The global scaling hypothesis is

\[
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
\approx
\alpha\,
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V),
\]

where \(\alpha\) is a single multiplicative factor. The fitted factor was
estimated by least squares through the origin over the robust voltage domain,

\[
\alpha
=
\frac{
\sum_i
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V_i)
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V_i)
}{
\sum_i
\overline{\dot V}_{5}^{,2}(V_i)
}.
\]

No additive intercept was introduced because the quantity of interest is a
pure temporal-rate scaling between trajectories; an offset would correspond to
a nonzero discharge-rate difference even when the reference rate vanished.

The fitted value is compared with the resistive-load ratio

\[
\alpha_R
=
\frac{R_{5}}{R_{3.33}}
=
\frac{5}{3.33},
\]

which is the current ratio expected at equal terminal voltage under ideal
resistive loading.

** Local dynamic ratio

A single global factor does not test whether the scaling is locally uniform
along the discharge trajectory. The voltage-dependent local dynamic ratio was
therefore defined as

\[
K(V)
=
\frac{
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
}{
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V)
}.
\]

If the two mean discharge-rate fields differed only by a global temporal
rescaling, \(K(V)\) would remain constant and equal to \(\alpha\).
Systematic voltage dependence of \(K(V)\) therefore measures local departure
from the global scaling relation.

For interpretation in relative terms, the local departure from the global
factor is also expressed as

\[
\delta_K(V)
=
100\left[
\frac{K(V)}{\alpha}-1
\right]\,\%.
\]

** Robust voltage interval

The ratio \(K(V)\) becomes numerically unstable where either group mean
approaches zero because small denominator values amplify residual estimation
errors. A rate-magnitude criterion was therefore applied before fitting or
interpreting the ratio.

A voltage-grid point was considered robust when both group-mean derivative
magnitudes satisfied

\[
\left|
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
\right|
\ge \dot V_{\min}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\left|
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V)
\right|
\ge \dot V_{\min},
\]

with the default threshold

\[
\dot V_{\min}=2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}.
\]

The principal analysis uses the longest contiguous voltage interval satisfying
this criterion. This avoids interpreting isolated valid points separated by
near-zero-rate regions and makes the domain of the global and local scaling
analyses explicit.

** Bootstrap uncertainty and monotone estimate

Uncertainty in the local scaling function was quantified by nonparametric
bootstrap resampling of the repeated discharges. For each bootstrap replicate,
the five \(3.33\,\Omega\) runs and the five \(5\,\Omega\) runs were
resampled independently with replacement. Group-mean rate fields were
recomputed, their ratio was formed on the fixed robust voltage interval, and
the procedure was repeated 2000 times. Pointwise 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles
define the reported 95% bootstrap confidence band.

Because the exploratory analyses showed that the large-scale trend in \(K(V)\)
is predominantly decreasing with voltage, the final descriptive estimate uses a
non-increasing isotonic regression fitted by the pool-adjacent-violators
algorithm. The constraint is used only to summarize the broad monotone trend; it
is not taken as evidence for discrete physical regimes.

To account approximately for repeat-dependent precision, the isotonic fit was
weighted by the inverse squared delta-method standard error of the ratio of the
two group means. Extremely large weights can occur where the estimated standard
error is very small, so the weights were capped at their 95th percentile before
the monotone fit. The bootstrap confidence interval itself is obtained by
resampling repeated discharges rather than from the isotonic-fit weights.

** Sensitivity to processing parameters

The dependence of the local dynamic ratio on processing choices was assessed
systematically rather than by selecting a single visually favourable
configuration. The Savitzky--Golay window was varied over

\[
30,\ 60,\ 120\,\mathrm{s},
\]

and the minimum derivative threshold over

\[
1\times10^{-4},\
2\times10^{-4},\
3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}.
\]

All nine combinations were evaluated using the same voltage-domain procedure.
Agreement of \(K(V)\) across these combinations is used as a robustness check
for the observed voltage dependence. The default values
\(60\,\mathrm{s}\) and
\(2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}\) are retained for the main
quantitative summaries.

** Sequential-order and leave-one-out analyses

Because the two load conditions were acquired sequentially rather than
interleaved, additional analyses were used to quantify sensitivity to possible
run-order effects.

Within each resistance group, the measured capacity was regressed against run
order. The same approach was applied pointwise to the voltage-rate trajectories:
for each common voltage value, a linear regression of \(\dot V\) against
within-group run index was used to estimate the first-to-last equivalent change
across the five repeated discharges. These regressions are diagnostic rather
than corrective; no run-order term is subtracted from the data.

The possible magnitude of within-group dynamic drift was compared with the
inter-group residual after global scaling,

\[
r(V)
=
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
-
\alpha\,
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V).
\]

Finally, stability of the fitted global factor was assessed by leave-one-out
resampling. One run was omitted from the \(3.33\,\Omega\) group and one
from the \(5\,\Omega\) group, producing all

\[
5\times5=25
\]

paired leave-one-out combinations. The global factor \(\alpha\) was
re-estimated for every combination using the same robust-threshold and
least-squares procedure. The resulting distribution measures sensitivity of the
global scaling estimate to the finite set of repeated discharges.
