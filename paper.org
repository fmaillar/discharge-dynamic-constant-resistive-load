* COMMENT Locked scientific scope

** Central question

Do archived Li-ion discharges obtained under two constant resistive loads admit
a simple global temporal-rate scaling, and how does the local voltage-rate
scaling vary along the discharge trajectory?

** Primary result to test and report

For the two discharge families,

\[
\left(\frac{dV}{dt}\right)_{3.33\,\Omega}
\approx
\alpha
\left(\frac{dV}{dt}\right)_{5\,\Omega},
\]

with the fitted global factor experimentally equal to the load-resistance ratio,

\[
\alpha \simeq \frac{5}{3.33}.
\]

The present analysis gives \(\alpha=1.498019\) and
\(5/3.33=1.501502\), a relative difference of approximately 0.23%.

** Local quantity

The local dynamic ratio is

\[
K(V)=
\frac{
\left(dV/dt\right)_{3.33\,\Omega}
}{
\left(dV/dt\right)_{5\,\Omega}
}.
\]

The central secondary observation is that \(K(V)\) is not constant over the
robust voltage interval even though the global scale factor follows the
resistance ratio.

** Claims allowed by the archived data

- A global scaling factor can be estimated reproducibly from the two discharge
  families.
- The global factor is experimentally indistinguishable, at the precision
  relevant here, from the ratio of load resistances.
- The local dynamic ratio varies systematically with voltage.
- The form of the local ratio is robust to the tested smoothing windows and
  derivative thresholds.
- Bootstrap and leave-one-out analyses can quantify repeatability and
  sensitivity to the finite set of repeated discharges.

** Claims explicitly excluded

- The local variation of \(K(V)\) is not attributed uniquely to load
  resistance because load condition and acquisition order are confounded.
- No microscopic electrochemical mechanism is identified from these data alone.
- No universal scaling law across cell chemistries, temperatures, ageing states,
  or load families is claimed.
- =current_a= is not treated as an independent measurement; for these processed
  files it is derived from terminal voltage and the known resistive load.
- The work does not revisit the harmonic-fluctuation, aliasing, or low-SOC
  spectral claims of the previous article.

** Separation from the previous article

The previous article studied fast detrended voltage fluctuations, harmonic
aliasing, phase locking, periodic reconstruction, and low-SOC spectral
organization. The present manuscript uses the slow discharge trajectory and its
voltage-rate field. Harmonic spectra, aliased lines, periodic reconstruction,
and spectral-noise conclusions are outside the present contribution.

** Frozen headline numerical results

The fitted global scaling factor is

\[
\alpha=1.498019,
\]

while the load-resistance ratio is

\[
\alpha_R=\frac{5}{3.33}=1.501502.
\]

Their relative difference is \(-0.2320\%\). For manuscript interpretation,
this difference is treated as negligible at the precision of the experiment:
the global voltage-rate scaling is therefore reported as experimentally equal to
the resistance ratio.

The mean discharge-duration ratio is

\[
T_{5\Omega}/T_{3.33\Omega}=1.479226.
\]

The monotone local dynamic ratio spans

\[
0.428024 \le K(V) \le 2.035410
\]

over the robust interval and crosses the global factor at

\[
V=2.171871\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

The RMS local departure from the global factor is \(22.549\%\), and the

departures (+35.873% at 1.393129 V and -71.427% at 3.125032 V) are retained as

** Frozen robustness results

The shape of \(K(V)\) is insensitive to the tested processing combinations:
Savitzky--Golay windows of 30, 60, and 120 s crossed with rate thresholds of
\(1\times10^{-4}\), \(2\times10^{-4}\), and
\(3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}\).

The exploratory unconstrained-bootstrap analysis found that the pointwise 95%

the two broad continuous intervals 1.393129--1.963029 V and

* Experimental data and acquisition

** Discharge data set

Ten archived discharge records, D70--D79, were analysed. They originate from
the same experimental campaign as the previously reported measurements but are
used here for a different purpose: characterization of the slow discharge
trajectory and its voltage-rate scaling. D70--D74 were acquired with a
\(3.33\,\Omega\) resistive load and D75--D79 with a
\(5\,\Omega\) load, providing five repetitions per load condition. All ten
records were sampled at \(f_s=10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), and the nominal reference
capacity stored in the archived metadata is \(2.6\,\mathrm{Ah}\).

Time and reconstructed terminal voltage are the primary observables used in the
present analysis. The archived records also contain state of charge and a
derived current field, but neither is required as the independent coordinate for
the scaling analysis. Instead, discharge trajectories are compared at common
terminal-voltage values.

#+name: tab:dataset
#+caption: Archived discharge records used in the present study.
| Runs     | Load resistance      | Repeats | Sampling              |
|----------+----------------------+---------+-----------------------|
| D70--D74 | \(3.33\,\Omega\) |       5 | \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\) |
| D75--D79 | \(5\,\Omega\)    |       5 | \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\) |

The experimental scope is deliberately limited to these ten archived records.
No additional cell types, temperatures, ageing states, or loading protocols are
introduced; conclusions therefore apply only to the two discharge families
described in Table [[tab:dataset]].

** Measurement chain

Terminal voltage was acquired with a Keithley 2182 nanovoltmeter. In the
processed archive, the measured battery-voltage channel had already been
converted to terminal voltage using the divider gain associated with each
record; for D70--D79 this gain is 500. The present analysis therefore uses the
archived reconstructed terminal-voltage signal directly and does not attempt a
new reconstruction from raw instrument counts.

Only acquisition parameters that are explicitly supported by the archived data
and metadata are reported. The surviving material does not permit an
unambiguous reconstruction of every instrument setting used during D70--D79,
and auxiliary-script settings are not assumed to apply unless independently
supported by the archived records. This avoids introducing undocumented
instrument parameters into the analysis.

** Constant-resistance loading and derived current

The experimental condition is the external resistive load,
\(R_L=3.33\,\Omega\) or \(R_L=5\,\Omega\). For an ideal resistive load,

\[
I(t)=\frac{V(t)}{R_L}.
\]

The archived =current_a= field is not an independent current measurement: it was
computed from terminal voltage and the known load resistance. It is therefore
not used as an independent observable. In particular, no inference is based on
voltage--current correlation because such a relation is imposed algebraically by
the reconstruction.

At a common terminal voltage, the ideal resistive-load current ratio is

\[
\frac{I_{3.33}}{I_{5}}
=
\frac{V/3.33}{V/5}
=
\frac{5}{3.33}.
\]

This provides the natural first-order reference for the global voltage-rate
scaling tested below.

** Sequential acquisition design

The two load conditions were acquired sequentially rather than in an
interleaved or randomized order: D70--D74 correspond to
\(3.33\,\Omega\), followed by D75--D79 at \(5\,\Omega\). Load
condition and acquisition order are therefore confounded.

This design permits direct comparison of the two archived discharge families
but limits causal interpretation of local differences between them. A
voltage-dependent difference cannot be assigned uniquely to load resistance if
an unobserved sequential change in the experimental system could generate an
effect of comparable magnitude.

Accordingly, the analysis distinguishes between a global scaling relation,
which can be estimated directly from the two families, and local
voltage-dependent departures from that relation, which are treated as empirical
differences between the archived groups. Possible order effects are quantified
with within-group trend diagnostics and leave-one-out analyses, but no
run-order correction is imposed on the data.

* Methods

** Voltage smoothing and rate estimation

For each discharge, the terminal-voltage signal \(V(t)\) was smoothed with a
third-order Savitzky--Golay filter using a default window of
\(60\,\mathrm{s}\). At \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\), the implementation uses
601 samples after enforcing the odd window length required by the filter. The
voltage derivative was evaluated directly from the differentiated polynomial
fit, using the physical sampling interval \(\Delta t=1/f_s\),

\[
\dot V(t)=\frac{dV}{dt}.
\]

This avoids a separate finite-difference operation on the smoothed signal. To
limit edge effects, half of the filter-window length was discarded at each end
before interpolation. The analysis targets the slow discharge trajectory only;
no detrended fast residual or spectral decomposition is used.

** Transformation to a common voltage coordinate

Because the two load conditions produce different discharge durations, records
were not compared pointwise in time. Instead, each processed trajectory was
expressed as \(\dot V(V)\). For every discharge, smoothed-voltage samples
were sorted by voltage and the corresponding derivative values were
interpolated onto 1000 equally spaced points spanning the voltage interval
common to all ten records. Duplicate voltage values were collapsed before
interpolation.

For load group \(R\), the five interpolated trajectories were averaged
pointwise,

\[
\overline{\dot V}_{R}(V)
=
\frac{1}{N_R}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_R}
\dot V_{R,j}(V),
\qquad N_R=5.
\]

This representation compares the two groups at equal terminal voltage while
remaining independent of their different time axes.

** Global voltage-rate scaling

The global scaling model is

\[
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
\approx
\alpha\,
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V),
\]

with a single multiplicative factor \(\alpha\). It was estimated by
least-squares regression through the origin over the robust voltage interval,

\[
\alpha
=
\frac{
\sum_i
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V_i)
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V_i)
}{
\sum_i
\overline{\dot V}_{5}^{\,2}(V_i)
}.
\]

No intercept was included because the hypothesis being tested is a pure
temporal-rate rescaling. The fitted factor was compared with the resistive-load
ratio

\[
\alpha_R
=
\frac{R_5}{R_{3.33}}
=
\frac{5}{3.33},
\]

which is the ideal current ratio at equal terminal voltage.

** Local dynamic ratio

Local departure from the global scaling model was characterized by

\[
K(V)
=
\frac{
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
}{
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V)
}.
\]

Under exact global rescaling, \(K(V)\) would be constant and equal to
\(\alpha\). Its relative departure from the global factor was therefore
expressed as

\[
\delta_K(V)
=
100\left[
\frac{K(V)}{\alpha}-1
\right]\,\%.
\]

** Robust voltage domain

The ratio becomes unstable where either mean voltage rate approaches zero.
Accordingly, a grid point was retained only when

\[
\left|
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
\right|
\ge \dot V_{\min}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\left|
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V)
\right|
\ge \dot V_{\min},
\]

with

\[
\dot V_{\min}=2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}.
\]

The principal analysis used the longest contiguous interval satisfying this
criterion. This prevents isolated near-zero-rate points from dominating the
ratio and fixes a common domain for global and local comparisons.

** Bootstrap uncertainty and monotone summary

Uncertainty in \(K(V)\) was estimated by nonparametric bootstrap resampling.
The five runs in each resistance group were sampled independently with
replacement, group-mean rate fields were recomputed, and their ratio was
evaluated over the fixed robust interval. This procedure was repeated 2000
times. Pointwise 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles define the reported 95% bootstrap
confidence band.

A weighted non-increasing isotonic regression, computed with the
pool-adjacent-violators algorithm, was used to summarize the broad trend in
\(K(V)\). The monotonic constraint is descriptive and is not interpreted as
evidence for discrete physical regimes. Weights were proportional to the
inverse squared delta-method standard error of the ratio of group means and were
capped at the 95th percentile to prevent a few very small estimated standard
errors from dominating the fit. Bootstrap uncertainty was obtained from
resampling the repeated discharges, not from the isotonic-regression weights.

** Processing-parameter sensitivity

Sensitivity to numerical processing choices was evaluated over all nine
combinations of Savitzky--Golay windows

\[
30,\ 60,\ 120\,\mathrm{s}
\]

and rate thresholds

\[
1\times10^{-4},\
2\times10^{-4},\
3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}.
\]

The same voltage-domain analysis was repeated for each combination. The
\(60\,\mathrm{s}\) window and
\(2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}\) threshold define the reference
analysis; the remaining combinations are used only to test robustness of the
observed \(K(V)\) dependence.

** Sequential-order diagnostics and leave-one-out analysis

The possible effect of sequential acquisition was assessed without applying any
correction to the data. Within each resistance group, measured capacity was
regressed against run order. At each common voltage value, \(\dot V\) was
also regressed against within-group run index to estimate the equivalent
first-to-last change across the five repetitions.

The resulting within-group drift magnitude was compared with the residual left
after global scaling,

\[
r(V)
=
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
-
\alpha\,
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V).
\]

Finally, sensitivity of \(\alpha\) to the finite repeat set was evaluated by
leaving out one run from each load group. All \(5\times5=25\) paired
leave-one-out combinations were analysed with the same robust-domain and
least-squares procedure.

* Results

** Normalized discharge trajectories

The two resistance groups followed closely related discharge trajectories despite
their different absolute durations. When time was normalized by the duration of
each record, the five \(3.33\,\Omega\) discharges and the five
\(5\,\Omega\) discharges showed a common overall voltage evolution, with
the largest visible departures concentrated toward the final voltage decline.
This establishes that the two experimental conditions traverse broadly similar
terminal-voltage trajectories but at different rates.

#+name: fig:normalized-trajectories
#+caption: Terminal-voltage trajectories for the two constant-resistance groups plotted against normalized discharge time. The five repeated records in each group show a common global shape, while deviations become more pronounced near the final voltage decline.
[[file:figures/screening/01_normalized_time.pdf]]

The mean discharge durations were approximately \(9239.9\,\mathrm{s}\)
for the \(3.33\,\Omega\) group and \(13667.9\,\mathrm{s}\) for the
\(5\,\Omega\) group, corresponding to a duration ratio

\[
\frac{T_{5\Omega}}{T_{3.33\Omega}}=1.479226.
\]

The duration ratio is therefore close to, but not identical with, the
resistance ratio. The remainder of the analysis compares the trajectories
locally in voltage rather than relying on duration alone.

** Global resistance scaling

Expressing the slow discharge dynamics as \(\dot V(V)\) reveals a strong
global multiplicative relation between the two load groups. A least-squares fit
through the origin over the robust voltage interval gives

\[
\alpha=1.498019.
\]

The corresponding load-resistance ratio is

\[
\frac{R_5}{R_{3.33}}
=
\frac{5}{3.33}
=
1.501502.
\]

The relative difference is only \(-0.2320\%\). At the precision relevant to
this experiment, the fitted global scaling factor is therefore experimentally
equal to the resistance ratio.

Although the global proportionality is extremely close to the ideal resistive
expectation, the residual is not featureless. Its smooth voltage dependence

** Voltage dependence of the local scaling factor

The local dynamic ratio

\[
K(V)
=
\frac{
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
}{
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V)
}
\]

varies substantially over the robust voltage interval
\(1.393129\)--\(3.125032\,\mathrm{V}\). The weighted monotone estimate
ranges from \(2.035410\) at the low-voltage end to \(0.428024\) at the
high-voltage end. It crosses the global factor at

\[
V=2.171871\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

#+caption: Local dynamic ratio \(K(V)\) over the robust voltage interval. [[file:figures/scaling_v4/12_monotone_ratio_bootstrap.pdf]]

Relative to the global factor, the RMS local departure is \(22.549\%\), and
the median absolute departure is \(17.053\%\). The largest endpoint

values are descriptive and are not interpreted as separate transition voltages.

Thus, the same data support two distinct statements: the two discharge families
obey an almost exact global scaling proportional to the resistance ratio, while
their local voltage-rate ratio is strongly voltage dependent.

** Robustness to processing choices and resampling

The observed voltage dependence of \(K(V)\) is not produced by a particular
choice of smoothing window or rate threshold. Repeating the analysis for
Savitzky--Golay windows of 30, 60, and 120 s and minimum-rate thresholds of
\(1\times10^{-4}\), \(2\times10^{-4}\), and
\(3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}\) produced closely overlapping
ratio curves over their common valid domains.

#+name: fig:parameter-sensitivity
#+caption: Sensitivity of the local dynamic ratio to Savitzky--Golay window length and minimum-rate threshold. The nine parameter combinations yield nearly coincident curves over the common voltage domain, showing that the broad voltage dependence of \(K(V)\) is not a tuning artifact.
[[file:figures/scaling_v3/11_parameter_sensitivity.pdf]]

Bootstrap resampling of the repeated discharges led to the same conclusion.
In the unconstrained bootstrap analysis, the pointwise 95% confidence interval
excluded the global \(\alpha\) over 58.32% of the robust voltage grid. The
two broad continuous exclusion intervals were 1.393129--1.963029 V and
2.690823--3.125032 V. An isolated one-grid-point exclusion near 2.525527 V was
not treated as physically meaningful.

The bootstrap band widens where repeat-to-repeat variability is larger, but the
large-scale decline of \(K(V)\) persists. The local departure from global
scaling is therefore reproducible at the level of the archived repeat set,
although its causal origin is considered separately below.

** Sensitivity to sequential acquisition order

The two resistance conditions were acquired sequentially, so possible
run-order effects were quantified explicitly. Measured capacity showed no
statistically significant monotonic trend within either group. For
\(3.33\,\Omega\), the fitted capacity slope was

\[
-0.00187951\,\mathrm{Ah/run},
\qquad p=0.169929,
\]

while for \(5\,\Omega\),

\[
+0.00098531\,\mathrm{Ah/run},
\qquad p=0.634002.
\]

Likewise, no voltage point in the robust interval showed a within-group
voltage-rate slope with \(p<0.05\) for either resistance group.

Nevertheless, the estimated magnitude of possible within-group drift is not
negligible compared with the local inter-group residual. The RMS magnitude of
the combined within-group drift estimate is

\[
4.2022\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}},
\]

whereas the RMS scaled inter-group residual is

\[
3.9107\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}.
\]

Their ratio is 1.0745. Thus, although no monotonic run-order trend is detected,
the available repeats do not allow the local voltage-dependent difference to be
attributed uniquely to load resistance.

[[file:figures/scaling_v5/18_drift_vs_scaling_residual.pdf]]

The global scaling factor itself is more stable. Across all 25 paired
leave-one-out combinations,

\[
\bar\alpha_{\mathrm{LOO}}=1.497934,
\qquad
s_{\alpha,\mathrm{LOO}}=0.048578,
\]

with a range of 1.394986--1.570954. The mean leave-one-out value is essentially
identical to the full-data estimate \(\alpha=1.498019\).

Taken together, these diagnostics support the global scaling result while
placing a clear limit on the interpretation of the local departure: the
voltage-dependent structure is reproducible as an empirical difference between
the two archived discharge families, but the sequential acquisition design does
not isolate resistance as its sole cause.

* Discussion

The principal result is the coexistence of two apparently contradictory
features: an almost exact global temporal-rate scaling and a strongly
voltage-dependent local departure from that scaling. The fitted factor
\(\alpha=1.498019\) differs from the load-resistance ratio
\(5/3.33=1.501502\) by only 0.232%. At the scale of the present experiment,
the global voltage-rate dynamics can therefore be regarded as proportional to
the resistance ratio. This is consistent with the first-order expectation for a
resistive load, for which the current at fixed terminal voltage scales as
\(1/R_L\).

The duration ratio, however, is not identical to the resistance ratio. The
mean discharge-duration ratio is 1.479226, whereas the global voltage-rate
factor is 1.498019. This difference is not surprising because total discharge
duration integrates the complete trajectory, including portions where the local
rate ratio departs from its global mean. A single duration ratio therefore
compresses voltage-dependent dynamics into one scalar quantity, whereas the
voltage-domain representation retains the local structure.

The local ratio \(K(V)\) demonstrates that the global scaling is not uniform
along the trajectory. Over the robust interval, the monotone estimate spans
0.428024--2.035410 and crosses the global factor near 2.172 V. The RMS local
departure from \(\alpha\) is 22.549%, substantially larger than the 0.232%
difference between \(\alpha\) and the nominal resistance ratio. Thus, the
near-exact global equality does not imply pointwise proportionality. Instead,
positive and negative local departures compensate sufficiently over the full
voltage domain to yield the observed global factor.

This distinction between global and local scaling is the central contribution
of the present analysis. A model based only on a constant time-rescaling factor
would reproduce the average discharge-rate relation but would miss systematic
voltage-dependent deviations. Conversely, focusing only on the local deviations
would obscure the remarkably simple global proportionality. Both levels are
needed to describe the archived data faithfully.

The local trend is not a numerical artifact of one particular processing
choice. The broad shape of \(K(V)\) remains nearly unchanged across the nine
tested combinations of Savitzky--Golay window and rate threshold. Independent
bootstrap resampling of repeated discharges also shows that the global factor is
excluded over broad parts of the robust voltage interval. These observations
support the interpretation of the voltage dependence as a reproducible property
of the archived repeat set rather than an accidental consequence of smoothing
or denominator thresholding.

The monotone representation should nevertheless be interpreted as a descriptive
summary rather than a mechanistic law. Isotonic regression intentionally
suppresses local reversals and therefore cannot establish discrete
electrochemical regimes. For the same reason, extrema of \(dK/dV\) and the
three-region segmentation explored during preliminary analysis were excluded
from the final interpretation. The present data support a broad
voltage-dependent departure from global scaling, not sharply defined transition
voltages.

A more important limitation arises from the experimental sequence itself. The
\(3.33\,\Omega\) discharges were acquired first and the
\(5\,\Omega\) discharges afterwards, so load condition and run order are
confounded. No statistically significant monotonic within-group trend was found
in capacity or voltage-rate slope, but the estimated combined within-group drift
has an RMS magnitude slightly larger than the scaled inter-group residual
(drift/residual ratio 1.0745). This means that the available repetitions do not
justify assigning the full local \(K(V)\) structure uniquely to load
resistance.

The appropriate interpretation is therefore asymmetric. The global result is
comparatively strong: the full-data \(\alpha\) is centred on the resistance
ratio, and the mean of the 25 leave-one-out estimates
(\(1.497934\)) is essentially unchanged. The local result is also reproducible
as an empirical difference between the two discharge families, but its causal
origin remains underdetermined by the sequential design. Uncontrolled changes
between the two acquisition blocks cannot be excluded with the archived data.

This limitation does not invalidate the scaling observation. It defines its
scope. The present study establishes that two sequential families of
constant-resistance discharges can exhibit a simple global scaling while
retaining substantial local voltage dependence. Demonstrating that the local
departure is caused specifically by resistance would require an interleaved or
randomized experiment, which is not available for this archived campaign.

The result is also deliberately narrower than the previous analysis of the same
experimental campaign. That work concerned detrended fast voltage fluctuations,
aliasing, harmonic organization, and low-SOC behaviour. Here the fast residual
is not used. The present contribution concerns the slow discharge trajectory and
the relation between \(\dot V\), terminal voltage, and resistive load. Using
the same raw measurements for these distinct questions does not require the two
phenomena to share a common mechanism.

From a modelling perspective, the data suggest a natural hierarchy. The
zeroth-order description is a global multiplicative law,

\[
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
\simeq
\frac{5}{3.33}
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V),
\]

which captures the dominant scaling. A more accurate empirical description
requires a voltage-dependent correction,

\[
\overline{\dot V}_{3.33}(V)
=
K(V)\,
\overline{\dot V}_{5}(V),
\]

with \(K(V)\) constrained here only by observation, not by a proposed
microscopic mechanism. This separation between a simple global law and a local
correction provides a compact way to summarize the discharge dynamics without
over-interpreting the available experiment.
